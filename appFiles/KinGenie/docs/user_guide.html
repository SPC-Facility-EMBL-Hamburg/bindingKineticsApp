KinGenie allows the processing, analysis and visualization of binding kinetics data.

Input files

Octet Biolayer Interferometry

Input one or many files with the '.frd' extension. These are XML-formatted files. Moreover, you can load the Method file with extension '.fmf'. All files corresponding to the same experiment should be selected together for loading the data. A complete example folder can be downloaded here.



Gator Biolayer Interferometry

Input the csv files with the traces data ('Assay_#_Channel#.csv') and the corresponding files describing the metadata (Setting.ini and ExperimentStep.ini). All files corresponding to the same experiment should be selected together for loading the data.



KinGenie simulation csv

If you performed a simulation for surface-based binding, you can export the results and import the csv file into KinGenie.


Processing kinetic traces

There are several options for processing the kinetics traces. If the operations are performed 'in-place', then the sensor traces will be modified. Otherwise, the sensor traces will remain unchanged, and the new traces will be generated.

  • Align the association phases: For each association step, KinGenie finds the previous step, computes the mean of the last ten points, and subtracts this value from the next steps.
  • Subtract baseline: One trace is selected and subtracted from other traces.
  • Average: The selected traces are averaged.
  • Inter-step correction (shift association): For each association step, KinGenie calculates the mean signal of the last n data points and the mean signal of the first n points of the following step. The difference between these two means is then subtracted from the association step.
  • Inter-step correction (shift dissociation): For each dissociation step, KinGenie finds the previous association step and computes the mean of the last n points (from one to ten). Then, the difference between this value and the mean of the first n points (from one to ten) is subtracted from the dissociation step.
  • Subtract experiment: Subtract compatible experiments sensor by sensor. The reference experiment may have the same number of sensors or a single reference sensor. Subtraction can use the complete experiment or only compatible interaction steps; the sampled time arrays must match the selected mode.

A common workflow would be to load the data, align the association phases, subtract the baseline, and then go to the analysis section. The inter-step correction is only necessary if there are big jumps due to buffer mismatch. For fast off rates, it is not recommended because data may be lost.

Surface-based binding

In this case, one of the interacting partners is attached to a surface (e.g., a sensor chip), and the other partner flows over the surface at a constant concentration.


Planning an experiment

Appropriate controls are essential to obtain useful data. Find below a list of recommendations based on David G. Myszka (1999).

1. Blank injection: Inject buffer alone over a surface with the ligand to identify signal artifacts from the injection process. The signal from the blank injection should be subtracted from the analyte signal.

2. Reference surface: Inject the analyte (at the highest concentration) over a surface without the ligand (or with a non-binding protein) to control for non-specific binding. Ideally, no signal change should be observed. To perform double-reference subtraction, this control should be performed for each analyte concentration. First subtract the reference-surface trace from the ligand-surface trace for each analyte injection. Then subtract a blank injection that has undergone the same reference-surface correction:

$$ S_{\text{double-ref}} = (S_{\text{ligand,analyte}}-S_{\text{reference,analyte}}) -(S_{\text{ligand,blank}}-S_{\text{reference,blank}}) $$

Correcting both injections against the reference surface avoids subtracting their shared background twice.

3. Analyte concentrations: Use a series of at least 5–7 analyte concentrations that span tenfold above and below the expected \( K_{\text{d}} \).

4. Mass transport limitation (MTL): Run the same analyte at different flow rates or ligand loading levels to verify that binding is not limited by diffusion. To minimize MTL effects, use a high flow rate and the lowest possible loading level that still provides a good signal-to-noise ratio.

5. Ligand activity: Confirm that the immobilized ligand is active and correctly captured by injecting a known positive control analyte.

6. Use oriented immobilization: Avoid random coupling procedures, such as those involving primary amines, which introduce surface heterogeneity. Prioritize capturing methods such as antibodies or fusion tags, that create homogeneous surfaces.


One to one

The chemical equation of the system is:

$$ \large{ A + B \rightleftharpoons AB } $$

where \( A \) is the attached molecule, \( B \) is the molecule flowing and \( AB \) is the complex. The signal \( S \) is proportional to the concentration of the complex, and follows the differential equation:

$$ \large{ \frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{K_{\text{d}}} [B] (S_{\text{max}} - S) - k_{\text{off}} S } $$

where \( k_{\text{off}} \) is the dissociation rate constant, \( K_{\text{d}} \) is the equilibrium dissociation constant, and \( S_{\text{max}} \) is the maximum signal (at infinite concentration of \( B \)). The association rate constant \( k_{\text{on}} \) is given by:

$$ \large{ k_{\text{on}} = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{K_{\text{d}}} } $$

During the dissociation step, the concentration of \( B \) is zero, simplifying the signal equation to:

$$ \large{ \frac{dS}{dt} = - k_{\text{off}} S } $$

References:

  • Schuck, P., & Zhao, H. (2010). The role of mass transport limitation and surface heterogeneity in the biophysical characterization of macromolecular binding processes by SPR biosensing. Surface plasmon resonance: methods and protocols, 15-54.


One to one (mass transport limitation)

The analyte moves between the bulk solution and the surface compartment, where it binds the immobilized molecule:

$$ B_{\text{bulk}} \rightleftharpoons B_{\text{surface}} $$ $$ B_{\text{surface}}+A \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}}}_{k_{\text{off}}}} AB $$

Here \( A \) is immobilized, \( B \) is the analyte, and the bulk concentration \( [B]_0 \) is prescribed by the experiment. Under the quasi-steady-state approximation, the signal during association obeys:

$$ \frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{k_{\text{on}}[B]_0 S_{\text{free}}-k_{\text{off}}S} {1+k_{\text{on}}S_{\text{free}}/k_{\text{tr},S}}, \qquad S_{\text{free}}=\max(S_{\text{max}}-S,0) $$

The free-capacity term equals \( S_{\text{max}}-S \) in the physical signal range. During dissociation, \( [B]_0=0 \), giving:

$$ \frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{-k_{\text{off}}S}{1+k_{\text{on}}S_{\text{free}}/k_{\text{tr},S}} $$

The association rate is \( k_{\text{on}}=k_{\text{off}}/K_d \). The fitted coefficient \( k_{\text{tr},S} \), reported as Ktr, has units of signal per micromolar per second. It differs from the physical transport rate \( k_{\text{tr},c} \), in inverse seconds: if \( S=\alpha[AB] \), then \( k_{\text{tr},S}=\alpha k_{\text{tr},c} \). The model is available for simulation and for fitting association and dissociation together.

References:

  • Schuck, P., & Zhao, H. (2010). The role of mass transport limitation and surface heterogeneity in the biophysical characterization of macromolecular binding processes by SPR biosensing. Surface plasmon resonance: methods and protocols, 15-54.

One to one (induced fit)

The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A + B \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}}}_{k_{\text{off}}}} AB_{\text{int}} } $$ $$ \large{ AB_{\text{int}} \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{c}}}_{k_{\text{rev}}}} AB } $$

where \( A \) is the attached molecule, \( B \) is the molecule flowing, \( AB_{\text{int}} \) is the intermediate complex, and \( AB \) is the induced complex. Regarding the signal, the set of non-redundant differential equations is:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS}{dt} &= k_{\text{on}}[B](S_{\text{max}}-S)-k_{\text{off}}(S-S_1) \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS_1}{dt} &= k_{\text{c}}S-(k_{\text{c}}+k_{\text{rev}})S_1 \end{aligned} $$

where \( S \) is the total bound signal, \( S_1 \) is the induced-complex contribution, and \( S_{\text{max}} \) is the total binding capacity. The intermediate and induced complexes are assumed to have the same signal coefficient. During the dissociation step, the concentration of \( B \) is zero, simplifying the differential equations to:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS}{dt} &= -k_{\text{off}}(S-S_1) \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS_1}{dt} &= k_{\text{c}}S-(k_{\text{c}}+k_{\text{rev}})S_1 \end{aligned} $$

One to one (conformational selection)

The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A_\text{inactive} \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{c}}}_{k_{\text{rev}}}} A_\text{active} } $$ $$ \large{ A_\text{active} + B \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}}}_{k_{\text{off}}}} AB } $$

where \( A_\text{inactive} \) is the attached molecule in the inactive state, \( A_\text{active} \) is the attached molecule in the active state (capable of binding \( B \)), \( B \) is the molecule flowing, and \( AB \) is the complex. Regarding the signal, the set of non-redundant differential equations is:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS_1}{dt} &= (-k_{\text{c}}-k_{\text{rev}})S_1-k_{\text{rev}}S+k_{\text{rev}}S_{\text{max}} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS}{dt} &= -k_{\text{on}}[B]S_1-(k_{\text{on}}[B]+k_{\text{off}})S+k_{\text{on}}[B]S_{\text{max}} \end{aligned} $$

where \( S \) is the signal from bound complex, \( S_1 \) is the signal-equivalent amount of inactive immobilized molecule, and \( S_{\text{max}} \) is the total binding capacity. During the dissociation step, the concentration of \( B \) is zero, simplifying the differential equations to:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS_1}{dt} &= (-k_{\text{c}}-k_{\text{rev}})S_1-k_{\text{rev}}S+k_{\text{rev}}S_{\text{max}} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS}{dt} &= - k_{\text{off}} S \end{aligned} $$

Note: This model is implemented for simulation purposes, but not for fitting experimental data.


Heterogeneous analyte

The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A + B_1 \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}_1}}_{k_{\text{off}_1}}} AB_1 } $$ $$ \large{A + B_2 \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}_2}}_{k_{\text{off}_2}}} AB_2} $$

where \( A \) is the attached molecule, \( B_1 \) and \( B_2 \) are two different species flowing, and \( AB_1 \) and \( AB_2 \) are the corresponding complexes. The signal \( S_i \) of each complex follows the differential equation:

$$ \large{ \frac{dS_i}{dt} = k_{on,i} F_i C_{TOT} S_{max,i} \left(1 - \sum_{j=1}^{2} \frac{S_j}{S_{max,j}}\right) - k_{off,i} * S_{i} } $$

where \( C_{TOT} \) is the total concentration of the analytes, \( F_{i} \) is the fraction of analyte \( i \) in the mixture, and \( S_{\text{max},i} \) is the maximum signal for analyte \( i \). The total signal \( S \) is the sum of the signals from each complex:

$$ \large{ S = S_1 + S_2 } $$

Note: This model is implemented for simulation purposes, but not for fitting experimental data.

References:

  • Gaudreault, Jimmy, et al. "Multi-temperature experiments to ease analysis of heterogeneous binder solutions by surface plasmon resonance biosensing." Scientific Reports 12.1 (2022): 14401.


Heterogeneous ligand

In this model, the immobilized ligand is represented by two independent binding site classes. The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A_1 + B \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}_1}}_{k_{\text{off}_1}}} A_1B } $$ $$ \large{ A_2 + B \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}_2}}_{k_{\text{off}_2}}} A_2B } $$

where \( A_1 \) and \( A_2 \) are two ligand site classes on the surface, \( B \) is the molecule flowing, and \( A_1B \) and \( A_2B \) are the corresponding complexes. The signal \( S_i \) of each complex follows the differential equation:

$$ \large{ \frac{dS_i}{dt} = \frac{k_{\text{off},i}}{K_{\text{d},i}} [B] (S_{\text{max},i} - S_i) - k_{\text{off},i} S_i } $$

where \( S_{\text{max},i} \) is the maximum signal for ligand site class \( i \). The fraction of site class 1 defines \( S_{\text{max},1} \), and the remaining fraction defines \( S_{\text{max},2} \). The total signal \( S \) is the sum of the signals from each complex:

$$ \large{ S = S_1 + S_2 } $$

During the dissociation step, the concentration of \( B \) is zero, simplifying the signal equation of each site class to:

$$ \large{ \frac{dS_i}{dt} = - k_{\text{off},i} S_i } $$

Note: This model is available for simulation and fitting of surface-based binding data. Steady-state fitting estimates \( K_{\text{d},1} \), \( K_{\text{d},2} \), the fraction of site class 1, and \( R_{\text{max}} \). Kinetic fitting is available for association and dissociation traces and additionally estimates \( k_{\text{off},1} \) and \( k_{\text{off},2} \).


Two-to-one

In this model, the ligand is assumed to have two binding sites. The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A + B \;\underset{k_{\text{off}}}{\overset{2k_{\text{on}}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; AB} $$ $$ AB + B \;\underset{\frac{2k_{\text{off}}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}}{\overset{\sqrt{\sigma}\,k_{\text{on}}}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; AB_2$$

where \( A \) is the attached molecule with two binding sites, \( B \) is the molecule flowing, \( AB \) is the complex with one bound analyte, and \( AB_2 \) is the complex with two bound analytes. The parameter \( \sigma \) is the cooperativity factor, which describes how the binding of the first analyte affects the binding of the second analyte. If \( \sigma > 1 \), there is positive cooperativity, if \( \sigma < 1 \), there is negative cooperativity, and if \( \sigma = 1 \), there is no cooperativity.

Regarding the fraction of \( AB \) (\( f_{AB} \)) and the fraction of \( AB_2 \) (\( f_{AB_2} \)), the set of non-redundant differential equations is:

$$ \large{ \frac{df_{AB}}{dt} = 2k_{\text{on}}\,[B] - \left(2k_{\text{on}}\,[B] + k_{\text{off}} + \sqrt{\sigma}\,k_{\text{on}}\,[B]\right) f_{AB} + \left(\frac{2k_{\text{off}}}{\sqrt{\sigma}} - 2k_{\text{on}}\,[B]\right) f_{AB_2} } $$ $$ \large{ \frac{df_{AB_2}}{dt} = \sqrt{\sigma}\,k_{\text{on}}\,[B]\, f_{AB} - \frac{2k_{\text{off}}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\, f_{AB_2} } $$

The total signal \( S \) is given by the sum of the signal produced by \( AB \) and \( AB_2 \):

$$ \large{ S = S_{max,AB} f_{AB} + S_{max,AB_2} f_{AB_2} } $$

where \( S_{\text{max},AB} \) is the maximum signal produced by the complex with one bound analyte, and \( S_{\text{max},AB_2} \) is the maximum signal produced by the complex with two bound analytes. The two sites have identical intrinsic binding rates. The singly and doubly bound states have separately specified or fitted response amplitudes.

During the dissociation step, the concentration of \( B \) is zero, simplifying the differential equations to:

$$ \large{ \frac{df_{AB}}{dt} = (-k_{\text{off}})\,f_{AB} + \left(\frac{2k_{\text{off}}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\right) f_{AB_2} } $$ $$ \large{ \frac{df_{AB_2}}{dt} = -\left(\frac{2k_{\text{off}}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\right) f_{AB_2} } $$

Note: This model is available for simulation and fitting of steady-state and combined association/dissociation data. Cooperativity can optionally be fitted using Fit sigma.

Solution-based binding

In this case, the interacting species are in solution and the concentrations of all species change over time. Solution data can also be fitted empirically with single and double exponential models. These models are useful for estimating observed relaxation rates without assuming a full mechanistic binding scheme.

One to one

The chemical equation of the system is:

$$ \large{ A + B \rightleftharpoons AB } $$

where \( A \) and \( B \) are the two interacting partners, and \( AB \) is the complex. The concentration of the complex follows the differential equation:

$$ \large{ \frac{d[\text{AB}]}{dt} = k_{\text{on}} [A] [B] - k_{\text{off}} [\text{AB}] } $$

where \( k_{\text{off}} \) is the dissociation rate constant, \( k_{\text{on}} \) is the association rate constant. The equilibrium dissociation constant \( K_{\text{d}} \) is given by:

$$ \large{ K_{\text{d}} = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}} } $$

One to one (induced fit)

The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A + B \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}}}_{k_{\text{off}}}} AB_{\text{int}} } $$ $$ \large{ AB_{\text{int}} \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{c}}}_{k_{\text{rev}}}} AB } $$

where \( A \) and \( B \) are the interacting molecules, \( AB_{\text{int}} \) is the intermediate complex, and \( AB \) is the induced complex. The set of non-redundant differential equations is:

$$ \large{ \frac{d[B]}{dt} = k_{\text{off}} ([B]_{\text{0}} - [B] - [AB]) - k_{\text{on}} ([B] - [B]_{\text{0}} + [A]_{\text{0}})[B] } $$ $$ \large{ \frac{d[AB]}{dt} = k_{\text{c}} ([B]_{\text{0}} - [B] - [AB]) - k_{\text{rev}} [AB] } $$

where \( [B]_{\text{0}} \) and \( [A]_{\text{0}} \) are the total concentrations of \( B \) and \( A \), respectively.

$$ \large{ [B]_{\text{0}} = [B] + [AB] + [AB_{\text{int}}] } $$ $$ \large{ [A]_{\text{0}} = [A] + [AB] + [AB_{\text{int}}] } $$

These rates describe small perturbations near equilibrium. In the induced-fit notation of Paul and Weikl (2016), their forward rate \( k_r \) corresponds to our \( k_c \), and their reverse rate \( k_e \) corresponds to our \( k_{\text{rev}} \). The smaller (dominant) relaxation rate is:

$$ \large{ k_{\text{obs,1}} = k_{\text{c}} + k_{\text{rev}} + \frac{\gamma}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{\gamma^2 + 4 k_{\text{off}} k_{\text{c}}}}{2} } $$

while the second relaxation rate is given by:

$$ \large{ k_{\text{obs,2}} = k_{\text{c}} + k_{\text{rev}} + \frac{\gamma}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{\gamma^2 + 4 k_{\text{off}} k_{\text{c}}}}{2} } $$

where,

$$ \large{ \gamma = -k_{\text{c}} - k_{\text{rev}} + k_{\text{off}} + k_{\text{on}} (\delta - K_\text{D}^{\text{app}}) } $$ $$ \large{ \delta = \sqrt{(\text{[B]}_{\text{0}} - \text{[A]}_{\text{0}} + K_\text{D}^{\text{app}})^2 + 4 \text{[A]}_{\text{0}} K_\text{D}^{\text{app}}} } $$ $$ \large{ K_\text{D}^{\text{app}} = \frac{k_{\text{off}} k_{\text{rev}}}{k_{\text{on}} (k_{\text{c}} + k_{\text{rev}})} } $$

References:

  • Paul, F., & Weikl, T. R. (2016). How to distinguish conformational selection and induced fit based on chemical relaxation rates. PLoS computational biology, 12(9), e1005067.


One to one (conformational selection)

The chemical equations of the system are:

$$ \large{ A_\text{inactive} \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{c}}}_{k_{\text{rev}}}} A_\text{active} } $$ $$ \large{ A_\text{active} + B \mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}\limits^{k_{\text{on}}}_{k_{\text{off}}}} AB } $$

where \( A_\text{inactive} \) is the 'receptor' molecule in the inactive state, \( A_\text{active} \) is the 'receptor' molecule in the active state (capable of binding \( B \)), \( B \) is the ligand, and \( AB \) is the complex. The set of non-redundant differential equations is:

$$ \large{ \frac{d}{dt}[A_{\text{active}}] = k_{\text{c}} ([A]_{\text{0}} - [A_{\text{active}}]) - (k_{\text{rev}} + k_{\text{on}}[B])[A_{\text{active}}] + (k_{\text{off}} - k_{\text{c}})([B]_{\text{0}} - [B]) } $$ $$ \large{ \frac{d}{dt}[B] = k_{\text{off}} ([B]_{\text{0}} - [B]) - k_{\text{on}}[A_{\text{active}}][B] } $$

where \( [B]_{\text{0}} \) and \( [A]_{\text{0}} \) are the total concentrations of \( B \) and \( A \), respectively.

$$ \large{ [B]_{\text{0}} = [B] + [AB] } $$ $$ \large{ [A]_{\text{0}} = [A]_{\text{inactive}} + [A]_{\text{active}} + [AB] } $$

These rates describe small perturbations near equilibrium. For conformational selection, Paul and Weikl's \( k_e \) corresponds to our forward rate \( k_c \), and their \( k_r \) to our reverse rate \( k_{\text{rev}} \). The smaller (dominant) relaxation rate is:

$$ \large{ k_{\text{obs,1}} = k_{\text{c}} + \frac{\alpha}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{\alpha ^2 + \beta}}{2} } $$

while the second relaxation rate is given by:

$$ \large{ k_{\text{obs,2}} = k_{\text{c}} + \frac{\alpha}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha ^2 + \beta}}{2}} $$

where,

$$ \large{ \begin{aligned} \alpha ={}& k_{\text{rev}}-k_{\text{c}} \\ &+\frac{k_{\text{off}}\left[(2k_{\text{c}}+k_{\text{rev}})\delta +k_{\text{rev}}([B]_0-[A]_0-K_D^{\text{app}})\right]} {2k_{\text{c}}K_D^{\text{app}}} \end{aligned} } $$ $$ \large{ \beta = 2k_{\text{rev}}\left[2k_{\text{c}}-k_{\text{off}} -\frac{k_{\text{off}}(\delta-[B]_0+[A]_0)}{K_D^{\text{app}}}\right] } $$ $$ \large{ \delta = \sqrt{(\text{[B]}_{\text{0}} - \text{[A]}_{\text{0}} + K_\text{D}^{\text{app}})^2 + 4 \text{[A]}_{\text{0}} K_\text{D}^{\text{app}}} } $$ $$ \large{ K_\text{D}^{\text{app}} = \frac{k_{\text{off}}(k_{\text{c}}+k_{\text{rev}})}{k_{\text{on}}k_{\text{c}}} } $$

References:

  • Paul, F., & Weikl, T. R. (2016). How to distinguish conformational selection and induced fit based on chemical relaxation rates. PLoS computational biology, 12(9), e1005067.


Fitting

KinGenie supports the following fitting methods for surface-based binding data: The steady-state data can be analysed with the one-to-one, two-to-one, and heterogeneous ligand models. The kinetic data can be analysed with the one-to-one, one-to-one mass transport limitation (MTL), one-to-one induced fit, two-to-one, and heterogeneous ligand models. Fitting the surface-based steady-state provides equilibrium dissociation constants and signal amplitudes. Fitting the surface-based kinetic data also provides dissociation rate constants. Regarding solution-based kinetic data, it can be fitted with single and double exponential models, as well as one binding site and induced-fit models.

Fitting minimizes the sum of squared residuals between the data and model predictions. One-site models use analytical responses. Surface induced-fit and two-site kinetics use matrix exponentials; heterogeneous ligand responses are sums of analytical one-site responses. Surface MTL and heterogeneous analyte simulations use numerical ODE integration, as do solution induced-fit and conformational-selection simulations. When creating a new dataset for analysis, traces with the same sample ID are fitted with global parameters (e.g., \( K_{\text{d}} \) , \( k_{\text{off}} \)). If the option 'Smax Linked By Sensor' is activated when doing the fitting, the \( S_{\text{max}} \) parameter is shared between traces with the same sample ID and Smax_ID. The editable Smax_ID defines a capacity group; replicate number alone does not determine sharing. Link traces only when they have comparable active binding capacity.

Asymmetric 95% confidence intervals are available for the one-site model: they are calculated automatically for steady-state fits, and using 'e. Confidence interval' for combined association/dissociation fits without mass transport. These profile intervals are distinct from covariance-based parameter errors and are not provided for every model. Please remember that low error values do not necessarily mean that the selected model is the one describing the underlying data generating process. For example, if the binding process follows a one-to-one with MTL model, the fitted parameters of a one-to-one model (without MTL) may have low errors, but be still far-off from the true values.


Observed constants

When fitting the association phase with the one-to-one binding model, a plot of the observed constants versus ligand concentration is provided. The observed constants are fitted independently for each trace, using a single exponential function:

$$ \large{ Y = a0 + a1*\exp(-k_{\text{obs}} t) } $$

In the ideal case, the observed constants should be linear with the ligand concentration.


Suggestions (surface-based binding)

Once the fitting is done, it is important to check the fitted curves and the fitted parameters. The constants should be within expected ranges, and the signal amplitude ($S_{\text{max}}$) should be reasonable. In the ideal case, if a local per-curve ($S_{\text{max}}$) is used, the fitted $S_{\text{max}}$ should be similar for all curves that belong to biosensors that were loaded with the same amount of ligand. Moreover, the fitting of more complex models should be done with caution, unless there is a strong mechanistic reason to do so. As suggested in www.sprpages.nl, it is important to verify that the ligand and the analyte are pure and homogenous; the association phase has enough curvature; the dissociation phase is long enough; there is no buffer mismatch; the analyte concentration range is wide enough; the behaviour is reproducible.
